## ABSTRACT

This paper addresses the optimal output tracking problem for a class of systems with unknown dynamics and specified convergence rates. We propose an adaptive optimal control method based on the adaptive dynamic programming (ADP) method, which solely relies on input-output data. Specifically, we re-express the system state based on input-output data and design the dynamic feedback controller accordingly. Subsequently, we propose two ADP algorithms based on policy iteration and value iteration to determine the optimal control gain and provide the conditions for ensuring the convergence of both algorithms under a given tracking error convergence rate. Finally, the simulation study results demonstrate the proposed method's effectiveness.

## 1. Introduction

The output tracking problem addresses the design of a feedback controller that achieves asymptotic tracking of a class of reference inputs while ensuring the stability of the closed-loop system. This represents a general mathematical formulation with broad practical implications across various domains, such as aircraft $[1]$ , satellites $[2]$ , robotic manipulators $[3]$ , and vehicles $[4]$ . It is common practice to achieve optimal output tracking performance using the linear quadratic regulator (LQR) to determine the optimal gain for state feedback control. A critical step in this process involves solving the algebraic Riccati equation (ARE), an area of significant research interest $[5–7]$ . Amongst various methods employed, the policy iteration (PI) algorithm proposed by Kleinman $[8]$ remains one of the most widely used approaches. However, solving the output regulation equation and using LQR for optimal controller design presuppose precise knowledge of the system dynamics. This idealized assumption can be challenging to satisfy in practical applications.

Alternative methods that do not require complete knowledge of system dynamics have been explored to address the above limitation. In recent years, methods based on machine learning and model-free adaptive control have become one of the research hotspots $[9–17]$ . However, methods based on adaptive dynamic programming (ADP) have remained popular, particularly following the work of Jiang et al. $[18]$ and Bian et al. $[19]$ , who built upon the PI algorithm and value iteration (VI) algorithm by incorporating the concept of ADP. Their methods enable iterative solutions to the ARE using only online state and input data, thereby eliminating the assumption of knowledge of system dynamics. Gao et al. $[20]$ applied this method to the problem of optimal output tracking to find the optimal solution for the output regulation equation and the ARE for CT systems. Jiang et al. $[21]$ extended this method to discrete-time (DT) systems and developed a VI version to broaden its applicability $[22]$ . Additionally, Xu et al. $[23]$ devised a mixed iteration algorithm that combines the advantages of VI and PI algorithms to address distributed optimal tracking control for multi-agent systems without requiring an initial stabilizing control gain and exhibiting faster convergence compared to traditional VI algorithms. Furthermore, Xie et al. $[24]$ designed an enhanced virtual system to replace the original system and its internal model, combining ADP methods with data from this enhanced virtual system to propose a new iterative learning equation to achieve optimal output regulation.

These approaches effectively circumvent the necessity for a system dynamics but typically presuppose accessibility to the system state. For instance, in $[24]$ , not only the system state but also its derivatives are required to construct the enhanced virtual system. This implies that practical applications necessitate real-time capability for measuring the system state variables.

Numerous researchers have devoted significant efforts to addressing the LQR problem in cases where the system dynamics are unknown, and the states are not directly accessible. Lewis et al. $[25]$ employed delayed input-output data to re-express the state of linear DT systems. Rizvi et al. $[26]$ utilized this state re-expression method to implement a model-free control scheme for optimal tracking. Similarly, Zhao et al. $[27]$ re-expressed the state of CT systems using delayed output data. Despite these advancements, it has been noted that the method proposed by Lewis et al. $[25]$ exhibits deadbeat characteristics $[28]$ , and the method in $[27]$ may lead to estimation bias after injecting exploration noise $[29]$ .

A different category of approaches involves the re-expression of system states using state observers. Zhu et al. $[30]$ employed an adaptive state observer to acquire knowledge of the full state, but this method requires partial knowledge of system dynamics and can only converge to a suboptimal solution. Recently, Rizvi et al. $[31]$ introduced a state parameterization method derived from the Luenberger observer design formula and the corresponding ADP method, which was subsequently extended to CT systems in $[29]$ . The extension of this approach to the output tracking problem has generated significant interest $[32–37]$ as it offers a practical solution to dealing with unknown system dynamics and inaccessible states. However, it is essential to note that this method may result in re-expressed states with high dimensionality, requiring more data collection and computational effort during the iterative process. Furthermore, these methods require sufficient running time for the system before applying the ADP method to ensure that re-expression errors are sufficiently small $[32]$ , posing challenges when combined with the VI algorithm due to potential divergence caused by initial control gains. Additionally, beyond asymptotic tracking, the convergence rate of closed-loop systems is also crucial in practical applications.

In this paper, we address the problem of achieving optimal output tracking control with a specified convergence rate. We propose a data-driven dynamic feedback controller and two methods for designing such a controller, which utilize system input-output data to determine the optimal control gain without relying on the system dynamics and states and guarantee optimal output tracking control at a specified convergence rate. The primary contributions of this study encompass:

(1) Propose a method of state re-expression based on reduced-order observer and prove the asymptotic equivalence relationship between the re-expressed state and the actual state. Compared to the method in $[29]$ , less computational effort is needed, and the dimension of the state after re-expression is smaller.

(2) Design a dynamic output feedback controller and propose an algorithm based on PI algorithms within the ADP framework for computing optimal control gain with assured convergence rate. Compared with the method of $[32]$ , which requires two exploration noises, the proposed algorithm only needs one and requires less data to be collected.

(3) Present the VI version of the above method, which overcomes the stringent assumption of the initial stabilizing control. Different from the method in $[29]$ , this algorithm can use output feedback control as the initial control policy, thus alleviating the problem of system output divergence.

The remaining sections of the paper are structured as follows: Section 2 introduces the problem statement and preliminary concepts. Section 3 explains the state re-expression method, analyses the re-expression error, and presents two corresponding ADP algorithms. Section 4 validates the effectiveness of these algorithms through simulations. Finally, conclusions drawn from this study are presented in Section 5.

## 2. Problem formulation and preliminaries

## 2.1. Problem formulation

Consider a class of CT linear time-invariant systems described as

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \dot {x} (t) = A x (t) + B u (t) \\ y (t) = C x (t) \end{array} \right.\tag{1}
$$

where $x(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the system state, $u(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$ and $y(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{p}$ are the control input and the output, respectively. The matrices $A \in R^{n \times n}$ , $B \in R^{n \times m}$ , and $C \in R^{p \times n}$ are unknown constant matrices.

Following the output regulation framework, the reference signal is assumed to be generated by a linear autonomous differential equation as follows:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \dot {v} (t) = D v (t) \\ r (t) = - E v (t) \end{array} \right.\tag{2}
$$

where $v(t) \in \mathbb{R}^q$ and $r(t) \in \mathbb{R}^p$ are the exosystem state and the reference signal, respectively. The matrices $D \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times q}$ and $E \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}$ are constant matrices.

Thus, the tracking error is given by

$$
e (t) = C x (t) + E v (t)\tag{3}
$$

For systems (1) and (2), we list the following assumptions:

Assumption 1 ([38]). The pair $(A, B)$ is stabilizable.

Assumption 2 ([38]). The matrix D has no eigenvalues with negative real parts.

Assumption 3 ([38]). rank $\left(\left[\begin{array}{cc}A-\lambda I_{n}&B\\C&0_{p\times m}\end{array}\right]\right)=n+p,\forall\lambda\in\sigma(D).$

Assumption 4. The pair $(A, C)$ is observable.

Remark 1. Assumptions 1–3 are standard assumptions for the linear output regulation problem and can be found in [38]. Assumption 4 is necessary to enable the output data to be used for re-expressing the system state in cases where the system state is not available.

We consider the following dynamic feedback controller:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \dot {z} (t) = G _ {1} z (t) + G _ {2} e (t) \\ u (t) = - K _ {x} x (t) - K _ {z} z (t) \end{array} \right.\tag{4}
$$

where the matrix pair $\left(G_{1}, G_{2}\right)$ with $G_{1} \in \mathbb{R}^{pq \times pq}$ , and $G_{2} \in \mathbb{R}^{pq \times p}$ is assumed to incorporate a minimal $p$ -copy internal model of matrix $D$ . $z(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{pq}$ is the state of the incorporate internal model. $K_{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ and $K_{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times pq}$ represent the control gain matrices.

The resulting closed-loop system formed by combining systems (1) and (2) is described as follows:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{c} \dot {x} _ {c} (t) = \bar {A} x _ {c} (t) + \bar {B} u (t) + \bar {G} v (t) \\ e (t) = \bar {C} x _ {c} (t) + E v (t) \end{array} \right.\tag{5}
$$

where $x_{c}(t) = \left[x^{\top}(t), z^{\top}(t)\right]^{\top}$ , $\bar{A} = \begin{bmatrix} A & 0_{n\times pq} \\ G_2C & G_1 \end{bmatrix}$ , $\bar{B} = \begin{bmatrix} B \\ 0_{pq\times m} \end{bmatrix}$ , $\bar{G} = \begin{bmatrix} 0_{n\times q} \\ G_2E \end{bmatrix}$ , and $\bar{C} = \left[C, 0_{p\times pq}\right]$ . Then, the controller can be written as $u(t) = -\bar{K}x_{c}(t)$ with $\bar{K} = \left[K_{x}, K_{z}\right]$ .

To proceed further, we introduce the following result:

Lemma 1 ([38]). Under Assumptions 1–3, if the pair $(G_{1}, G_{2})$ incorporates a p-copy internal model of matrix D, then the pair

$$
\left(\left[ \begin{array}{c c} A & 0 _ {n \times p q} \\ G _ {2} C & G _ {1} \end{array} \right], \left[ \begin{array}{c} B \\ 0 _ {p q \times m} \end{array} \right]\right)
$$

is stabilizable.

According to Lemma 1, the matrix pair $(\bar{A},\bar{B})$ in the system (5) is controllable. It implies that if the matrix $\bar{K}$ is chosen appropriately, then $(\bar{A} -\bar{B}\bar{K})$ can be Hurwitz. Moreover, there exists a unique matrix $X$ satisfying the following equation [38]:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \bar {G} = X D - (\bar {A} - \bar {B} \bar {K}) X \\ E = - \bar {C} X \end{array} \right.\tag{6}
$$

By defining the new variable $\bar{x}_c(t) = x_c(t) - Xv(t)$ , the system (5) can be equivalently expressed as follows:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \dot {\bar {x}} _ {c} (t) = \bar {A} \bar {x} _ {c} (t) + \bar {B} \bar {u} (t) \\ e (t) = \bar {C} \bar {x} _ {c} (t) \end{array} \right.\tag{7}
$$

where $\bar{u}(t) = -\bar{K}\bar{x}_c(t)$ .

Our aim is to design an optimal controller that ensures the tracking error $e(t)$ satisfies the following equation:

$$
\lim _ {t \to \infty} \mathrm{e} ^ {\gamma t} e (t) = 0\tag{8}
$$

where the convergence rate criterion $\gamma$ satisfies $0 \leq \gamma < \infty$ .

Under the control goal delineated in Eq. (8), we introduce transformed variables for the system state and tracking error. Specifically, let $\bar{x}_{c}^{\prime}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\gamma t}\bar{x}_{c}(t)$ and $e^{\prime}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\gamma t}e(t)$ . By doing so, we transform the system (7) into a new one, described as follows:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \dot {\bar {x}} _ {c} ^ {\prime} (t) = \bar {A} _ {\gamma} \bar {x} _ {c} ^ {\prime} (t) + \bar {B} \bar {u} ^ {\prime} (t) \\ e ^ {\prime} (t) = \bar {C} \bar {x} _ {c} ^ {\prime} (t) \end{array} \right.\tag{9}
$$

where $\bar{A}_{\gamma} = \bar{A} + \gamma I_{n + pq}$ and $\bar{u}'(t) = -\bar{K}\bar{x}_c'(t)$ .

From Lemma 1, it is known that the pair $(\bar{A}, \bar{B})$ is stabilizable, and according to the PBH test, it follows that

$$
\operatorname{rank} \left(\left[ \bar {A} - \lambda I _ {n + p q}, \bar {B} \right]\right) = n + p q \quad \forall \lambda \in \mathbb {C}
$$

Then, the following can be derived:

$$
\operatorname{rank} \left(\left[ \bar {A} _ {\gamma} - (\lambda + \gamma) I _ {n + p q}, \bar {B} \right]\right) = n + p q \quad \forall \lambda \in \mathbb {C}
$$

Therefore, the matrix pair $(\bar{A}_{\gamma},\bar{B})$ in the system (9) is stabilizable.

Building upon this foundation, to achieve optimal tracking with a specified convergence rate, the control gain $\bar{K}$ is designed based on the following optimal control problem:

Problem 1.

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \min _ {\bar {u} ^ {\prime} (\cdot)} & \int_ {t _ {0}} ^ {\infty} \left(\bar {x} _ {c} ^ {\prime \top} (\tau) Q \bar {x} _ {c} ^ {\prime} (\tau) + \bar {u} ^ {\prime \top} (\tau) R \bar {u} ^ {\prime} (\tau)\right) \mathrm{d} \tau \\ \text {s.t.} & \dot {\bar {x}} _ {c} ^ {\prime} (t) = \bar {A} _ {\gamma} \bar {x} _ {c} ^ {\prime} (t) + \bar {B} \bar {u} ^ {\prime} (t) \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

where $Q = Q^{\top} \geq 0$ , $R = R^{\top} > 0$ , and the pair $\left( \bar{A}_{\gamma}, \sqrt{Q} \right)$ is observable. In this paper, the matrix $Q$ is constructed as $Q = \begin{bmatrix} C^{\top}Q_{e}C & 0_{n\times pq}\\ 0_{pq\times n} & Q_{z} \end{bmatrix}$ with $Q_{e} > 0$ and $Q_{z} > 0$ . The matrix $Q_{z}$ is designed to ensure that the pair $\left( \bar{A}_{\gamma},\sqrt{Q} \right)$ is observable.

By defining $\bar{Q}_z = \begin{bmatrix} 0_{n\times n} & 0_{n\times pq}\\ 0_{pq\times n} & Q_z \end{bmatrix}$ , we reformulate the quadratic form of the state $\bar{x}_c'(t)$ as follows:

$$
\bar {x} _ {c} ^ {\prime \top} (t) Q \bar {x} _ {c} ^ {\prime} (t) = e ^ {\prime \top} (t) Q _ {e} e ^ {\prime} (t) + \bar {x} _ {c} ^ {\prime \top} (t) \bar {Q} _ {z} \bar {x} _ {c} ^ {\prime} (t)\tag{11}
$$

Remark 2. The influence of matrices $Q_{e}$ , $Q_{z}$ , and $R$ on the convergence rate of the system (7) is implicit and difficult to quantify directly. Introducing the parameter $\gamma$ explicitly specifies the exponential convergence rate of the tracking error, thereby addressing this limitation.

## 2.2. Preliminaries

Problem 1 is an instance of the LQR problem. According to linear optimal control theory, the optimal control gain $\vec{K}^*$ satisfies the following equation:

$$
\bar {K} ^ {*} = R ^ {- 1} \bar {B} ^ {\top} P ^ {*}\tag{12}
$$

Here, the matrix $P^{*}$ , which satisfies $P^{*} = P^{*\top} > 0$ , is the unique positive definite solution to the following ARE:

$$
\bar {A} _ {\gamma} ^ {\top} P ^ {*} + P ^ {*} \bar {A} _ {\gamma} + Q - P ^ {*} \bar {B} R ^ {- 1} \bar {B} ^ {\top} P ^ {*} = 0\tag{13}
$$

If the system dynamics are known, Eq. (13) can be solved using PI and VI algorithms, which are recalled below.

Lemma 2 ([8]). Let $\bar{K}^0\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times (n + pq)}$ be any stabilizing control gain, and the matrix $P^k$ be the solution of Lyapunov equation:

$$
\left(\bar {A} _ {\gamma} - \bar {B} \bar {K} ^ {k}\right) ^ {\top} P ^ {k} + P ^ {k} \left(\bar {A} _ {\gamma} - \bar {B} \bar {K} ^ {k}\right) + Q + \left(\bar {K} ^ {k}\right) ^ {\top} R \bar {K} ^ {k} = 0\tag{14}
$$

where for earch $k = 1,2,\ldots ,$

$$
\bar {K} ^ {k} = R ^ {- 1} \bar {B} ^ {\top} P ^ {k - 1}\tag{15}
$$

Then, the following properties hold:

1) $\sigma (\bar{A}_{\gamma} - \bar{B}\bar{K}^{k})\subset \mathbb{C}_{-};$

2) $P^{*} \leq P^{k+1} \leq P^{k};$

3) $\lim_{k\to \infty}P^k = P^*$ , $\lim_{k\to \infty}\bar{K}^k = \bar{K}^*$ .

Lemma 3 ([19]). Let $P^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{(n + pq) \times (n + pq)}$ be any real symmetric positive definite matrix and $i = 0$ . $P^{k+1}$ is the solution of following equation:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \tilde {P} ^ {k + 1} = P ^ {k} + \varepsilon^ {k} \Big (\bar {A} _ {\gamma} ^ {\top} P ^ {k} + P ^ {k} \bar {A} _ {\gamma} + Q - P ^ {k} \bar {B} R ^ {- 1} \bar {B} ^ {\top} P ^ {k} \Big) \\ P ^ {k + 1} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} P ^ {0} & \tilde {P} ^ {k + 1} \notin B ^ {i} \\ \tilde {P} ^ {k + 1} & \tilde {P} ^ {k + 1} \in B ^ {i} \end{array} \right. \end{array} \right.\tag{16}
$$

where the step size sequence $\varepsilon^k$ satisfies $\lim_{k\to \infty}\varepsilon^k = 0$ . $\left\{B^i\right\}_{i = 0}^\infty$ is a collection of bounded sets with nonempty interiors which satisfy $B^{i}\subseteq B^{i + 1}$ and $\lim_{i\to \infty}B^{i} = \mathcal{P}_{+}^{n}$ . The variable $i$ is determined by the following equation:

$$
i = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} i + 1 & \tilde {P} ^ {k} \notin B ^ {i} \\ i & \tilde {P} ^ {k} \in B ^ {i} \end{array} \right.
$$

Then, one has $\lim_{k\to \infty}P^k = P^*$ .

In this paper, since the system state $x(t)$ are not directly accessible, we propose a state re-expression method based on the following reduced-order observer in the subsequent sections.

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \dot {w} (t) = F w (t) + J y (t) + H u (t) \\ \hat {x} (t) = Q _ {1} y (t) + Q _ {2} w (t) \end{array} \right.\tag{17}
$$

where $w(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_w}$ with $n_w = n - p$ represents the observer state. $\hat{x}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the reconstructed state. $F \in R^{n_w \times n_w}$ , $J \in R^{n_w \times p}$ , $H \in R^{n_w \times m}$ , $Q_1 \in R^{n \times p}$ and $Q_2 \in R^{n \times n_w}$ are observer matrices.

Lemma 4 ([39]). Under Assumption 4, the necessary and sufficient conditions for Eq. (17) to serve as a state observer for the system (1) are as follows:

1) There exists a full-rank matrix $T \in \mathbb{R}^{n_w \times n}$ such that $L = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ T \end{bmatrix}$ is nonsingular;

2) All eigenvalues of matrix $F$ have negative real parts;

3) TA - FT = JC;

4) H = TB;

5) $\left[Q_1, Q_2\right] = L^{-1}$ .

Lemma 5 ([39]). For the system (1) and the reduced-order observer 17, assuming matrices A and F do not have any common eigenvalues, a necessary condition for the sylvester equation TA - FT = JC to admit a full-rank solution matrix T rendering $L = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ T \end{bmatrix}$ nonsingular is that the pair $(A, C)$ is observable and the pair $(F, J)$ is controllable.

Remark 3. To determine the matrices involved in the state observer defined by Eq. (17), knowledge of the dynamics of the system (1) remains essential. We address this issue in subsequent sections by further developing methods for re-expressing the system state based on Eq. (17).

## 3. Main results

## 3.1. State re-expression based on input-output data

Applying the Laplace transformation to Eq. (17) yields

$$
\begin{array}{l} w (s) = \left(s I _ {n _ {w}} - F\right) ^ {- 1} (J y (s) + H u (s) + w (0)) \\ = \frac {M (s)}{a (s)} (J y (s) + H u (s) + w (0)) \end{array}\tag{18}
$$

where $a(s) = s^{n_w} + a_1 s^{n_w - 1} + \cdots + a_{n_w - 1}s + a_{n_w}$ represents the characteristic polynomial of matrix F. $M(s) = M_1 s^{n_w - 1} + \cdots + M_{n_w - 1}s + M_{n_w}$ is the adjugate matrix of the characteristic matrix $(sI_{n_w} - F)$ . $a(s)$ and $M(s)$ satisfy the following relationship:

$$
\begin{array}{c} a (s) I _ {n _ {w}} = M (s) \Big (s I _ {n _ {w}} - F \Big) \\ = M _ {1} s ^ {n _ {w}} + \big (M _ {2} - M _ {1} F \big) s ^ {n _ {w} - 1} + \dots + \Big (M _ {n _ {w}} - M _ {n _ {w} - 1} F \Big) s - M _ {n _ {w}} F \end{array}
$$

Then

$$
M _ {i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} I _ {n _ {w}} & i = 1 \\ M _ {i - 1} F + a _ {i - 1} I _ {n _ {w}} & i = 2, \ldots , n _ {w} \end{array} \right.
$$

The $\frac{M(s)}{a(s)} Hu(s)$ in Eq. (18) can be rewritten as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {M (s)}{a (s)} H u (s) = \frac {1}{a (s)} \Big (M _ {1} H s ^ {n _ {w} - 1} + \dots + M _ {n _ {w} - 1} H s + M _ {n _ {w}} H \Big) u (s) \\ \qquad = \left[ M _ {n _ {w}} H, \ldots , M _ {1} H \right] \Big ([ s ^ {0}, \ldots , s ^ {n _ {w} - 1} ] ^ {\top} \otimes \frac {u (s)}{a (s)} \Big) \\ \qquad = \bar {M} _ {H} \zeta_ {u} (s) \end{array}\tag{19}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\mathrm{where} \bar {M} _ {H} = \left[ M _ {n _ {w}} H, M _ {n _ {w} - 1} H, \ldots , M _ {1} H \right] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {n _ {w} \times n _ {w} m} \mathrm{and} \zeta_ {u} (s) = [ s ^ {0}, \ldots , s ^ {n _ {w} - 1} ] ^ {\top} \otimes \frac {u (s)}{a (s)}.} \\ & {\quad \mathrm{Let} A _ {\zeta} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c c} 0 & 1 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & 1 \\ - a _ {n _ {w}} & - a _ {n _ {w} - 1} & \dots & - a _ {1} \end{array} \right] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {n _ {w} \times n _ {w}}, b = \left[ \begin{array}{c} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{array} \right] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {n _ {w}}. \mathrm{Then}} \\ & {\quad s \zeta_ {u} (s) = [ s, \ldots , s ^ {n _ {w}} ] ^ {\top} \otimes \frac {u (s)}{a (s)}} \\ & {\quad = \left(A _ {\zeta} [ s ^ {0}, \ldots , s ^ {n _ {w} - 1} ] ^ {\top} + [ 0, \ldots , 0, a (s) ] ^ {\top}\right) \otimes \frac {u (s)}{a (s)}} \\ & {\quad = \left(I _ {m} \otimes A _ {\zeta}\right) \zeta_ {u} (s) + \left(I _ {m} \otimes b\right) u (s)} \end{array}
$$

If $\zeta_{u}(0) = 0_{n_{w},m\times 1}$ , applying the inverse Laplace transform to the above equation yields

$$
\dot {\zeta} _ {u} (t) = \left(I _ {m} \otimes A _ {\zeta}\right) \zeta_ {u} (t) + \left(I _ {m} \otimes b\right) u (t)\tag{20}
$$

For $\frac{M(s)}{a(s)}Jy(s)$ , we do not handle it in the form of Eq. (20) as we do for $\frac{M(s)}{a(s)}Hu(s)$ , but rather consider the following equation:

$$
\dot {w} _ {1} (t) = F w _ {1} (t) + J y (t)\tag{21}
$$

Similarly, applying the Laplace transform to Eq. (21) yields

$$
w _ {1} (s) = \frac {M (s)}{a (s)} J y (s) + \frac {M (s)}{a (s)} w _ {1} (0)
$$

Then, the following can be derived:

$$
\frac {M (s)}{a (s)} J y (s) = w _ {1} (s) - \frac {M (s)}{a (s)} w _ {1} (0)\tag{22}
$$

Based on Eqs. (18)-(22), it can be derived that

$$

Lemma 6. Based on Lemma 4, for the state re-expression as shown in Eq. (23), the following statements holds.

Theorem 1. Considering the system (5), if $\bar{K}$ is a stabilizing control gain, the state driven by the state feedback controller $u_{c1}(t) = -\bar{K}x_c(t)$ asymptotically converges to the state driven by the re-expressed state feedback controller $u_{c2}(t) = -\bar{K}\bar{M}_{\bar{\zeta}}\bar{\zeta}(t)$ .

Proof. Denote the states driven by $u_{c1}(t)$ and $u_{c2}(t)$ as $x_{c1}(t)$ and $x_{c2}(t)$ , respectively. Then, it follows that

Since $(\bar{A}-\bar{B}\bar{K})$ is Hurwitz, and $\omega(t)$ converges zero asymptotically, according to the perturbation theory, $e_{x_{c}}$ is asymptotically stable. This completes the proof. ☐

## 3.3. VI-Based ADP algorithm using input-output data

Algorithm 1 requires an initial stabilizing control gain $\bar{K}_{Mc}^{0}$ that can be difficult to obtain due to the requirement for the convergence rate. In this subsection, we utilize the VI algorithm to update matrix $P$ , thereby bypassing the need for an initial stabilizing control gain.

From Eqs. (16) and (30), the equation can be written as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\rho^ {\prime \top} (t + \delta t) P ^ {k} \rho^ {\prime} (t + \delta t) - \rho^ {\prime \top} (t) P ^ {k} \rho^ {\prime} (t)} \\ & {\quad = \int_ {t} ^ {t + \delta t} \rho^ {\prime \top} (\tau) \Lambda^ {k} \rho^ {\prime} (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau - \int_ {t} ^ {t + \delta t} y ^ {\prime \top} (\tau) Q _ {e} y ^ {\prime} (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau} \\ & {\qquad - \int_ {t} ^ {t + \delta t} \rho^ {\prime \top} (\tau) \bar {Q} _ {z} \rho^ {\prime} (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau + \int_ {t} ^ {t + \delta t} 2 u _ {1} ^ {\prime \top} (\tau) R \bar {K} ^ {k + 1} \rho^ {\prime} (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau} \end{array}\tag{38}
$$

where $\Lambda^k = \bar{A}_{\gamma}^\top P^k +P^k\bar{A}_\gamma +Q.$

Upon further expansion, we get

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma \delta t} \bar {\zeta} _ {1} ^ {\top} (t + \delta t) \bar {P} ^ {k} \bar {\zeta} _ {1} (t + \delta t) - \bar {\zeta} _ {1} ^ {\top} (t) \bar {P} ^ {k} \bar {\zeta} _ {1} (t)} \\ & {\quad = \int_ {t} ^ {t + \delta t} \mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma (\tau - t)} \bar {\zeta} _ {1} ^ {\top} (\tau) \bar {\Lambda} ^ {k} \bar {\zeta} _ {1} (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau - \int_ {t} ^ {t + \delta t} \mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma (\tau - t)} y ^ {\top} (\tau) Q _ {e} y (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau} \\ & {\quad - \int_ {t} ^ {t + \delta t} \mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma (\tau - t)} \bar {\zeta} _ {1} ^ {\top} (\tau) \bar {Q} _ {M} \bar {\zeta} _ {1} (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau} \\ & {\quad + \int_ {t} ^ {t + \delta t} 2 \mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma (\tau - t)} u _ {1} ^ {\top} (\tau) R \bar {K} _ {M} ^ {k + 1} \bar {\zeta} _ {1} (t) \mathrm{d} \tau + \Omega_ {2} ^ {k} (t)} \end{array}\tag{39}

where $\bar{\Lambda}^k = \bar{M}_{\bar{\zeta}}^\top \Lambda^k\bar{M}_{\bar{\zeta}}\in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\bar{\zeta}}\times n_{\bar{\zeta}}}$ and $\Omega_2^k (t)$ is given by

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\Omega_ {2} ^ {k} (t) = 2 \omega^ {\top} (t) P ^ {k} \rho (t) - 2 \mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma \delta t} \omega^ {\top} (t + \delta t) P ^ {k} \rho (t + \delta t)} \\ & {\qquad + \mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma \delta t} \omega^ {\top} (t + \delta t) P ^ {k} \omega (t + \delta t) - \omega^ {\top} (t) P ^ {k} \omega (t)} \\ & {\qquad + \int_ {t} ^ {t + \delta t} 2 \mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma (\tau - t)} \omega^ {\top} (\tau) \big (\Lambda^ {k} - \bar {Q} _ {z} \big) \rho (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau} \\ & {\qquad - \int_ {t} ^ {t + \delta t} \mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma (\tau - t)} \omega^ {\top} (\tau) \big (\Lambda^ {k} - \bar {Q} _ {z} \big) \omega (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau} \\ & {\qquad + \int_ {t} ^ {t + \delta t} 2 \mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma (\tau - t)} \omega^ {\top} (\tau) \big (\bar {K} ^ {k + 1} \big) ^ {\top} R u _ {1} (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau} \end{array}
$$

Define

$$
\Phi_ {\bar {\zeta} u} ^ {k} = \left[ \int_ {t _ {0} ^ {k}} ^ {t _ {1} ^ {k}} \mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma (\tau - t _ {0} ^ {k})} \bar {\zeta} _ {1} (\tau) \otimes u _ {1} (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau , \dots , \int_ {t _ {s - 1} ^ {k}} ^ {t _ {s} ^ {k}} \mathrm{e} ^ {2 \gamma (\tau - t _ {s - 1} ^ {k})} \bar {\zeta} _ {1} (\tau) \otimes u _ {1} (\tau) \mathrm{d} \tau \right] ^ {\top}
$$

$$
\Phi_ {\Omega_ {2}} ^ {k} = \left[ \Omega_ {2} ^ {k} (t _ {0} ^ {k}), \dots , \Omega_ {2} ^ {k} (t _ {s - 1} ^ {k}) \right] ^ {\top}

Remark 8. Algorithm 2 eliminates the necessity for an initial stabilizing control gain for the system (30), but it also cause the state $\rho(t)$ of the system (29) to diverge, thereby violating the conditions stipulated in Lemma 8. One approach is to design a bound of $\rho(t)$ , such that when collecting data, if the state $\rho(t)$ exceeds this bound, $\rho(t)$ is reset to $\rho(0)$ and data collection continues [19]. However, this approach is not applicable to Algorithm 2. The presence of the preparatory phase means that resetting the $\rho(t)$ impacts the reexpression error $\omega(t)$ , leading to the failure of Algorithm 2. In response to this, we suggest designing the initial control gain in the form of $\bar{K}_{Mc}^{0} = \left[K_{y}, 0_{m \times n_{\omega}(m+1)}, K_{z}\right]$ with $K_{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p}$ . ## 4.3. Comparative experiments

Since Algorithm 2 employs the same set of data collected during $(t_0, t_s)$ at each iteration, and the duration of the preparation phase $t_0$ significantly influences the re-expression error $\omega(t)$ , which further affects the convergence performance of Algorithm 2. To evaluate this, we compared computational results under varying $t_0$ . As shown in Fig. 10, smaller $t_0$ yields larger $\left\| \bar{K}_{Mc}^k - \bar{K}_M^*\right\|$ , indicating more pronounced effects of $\omega(t)$ . Conversely, the impact diminishes with larger $t_0$ and eventually becomes negligible.

The compared simulation results with varying convergence rate criteria are depicted in Fig. 11. As the convergence rate criterion $\gamma$ increases, the tracking errors converge more rapidly. Fig. 12 shows the ITAE and ITSE under varying $\gamma$ and $Q_{e}$ . The introduction of $\gamma$ effectively suppresses the sensitivity of tracking performance to $Q_{e}$ . Under a constant $Q_{e}$ , increasing $\gamma$ can improve the tracking performance.

Finally, we compare our state re-expression method with that presented in $[29]$ . The comparative simulation results are presented in Fig. 13, it can be seen that the approach proposed in this paper achieves a faster convergence rate under the same eigenvalues. Furthermore, the proposed approach reduces the dimensionality of the re-expressed state by $(p-1)n+pm$ .

## 5. Conclusions

This paper addressed the optimal output tracking problem for CT linear time-invariant systems, with a particular focus on scenarios where the system dynamics and state are unknown and a specified convergence rate for the tracking error is required. The primary contribution of this study was to propose a method based on the reduced-order observer for re-expressing the state vector using input-output data. The proposed state re-expression method effectively reduced the dimension of the re-expressed state compared to existing methods. Moreover, a dynamic output feedback controller was designed, and two ADP-based algorithms were introduced in conjunction with PI and VI algorithms, respectively. These approaches eschewed reliance on the system dynamics and state information, instead utilizing online data to obtain the optimal control gain and ensure convergence rate with less data required compared to existing algorithms. Our future work is to design data-driven algorithms for adaptive optimal output tracking control of unknown nonlinear systems with a specified convergence rate of tracking error.
