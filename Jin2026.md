# Data-Driven Optimal Output Tracking of Electrostatic Micromirrors: A Guaranteed Convergence Rate Approach

This paper proposes a robust data-driven optimal output tracking scheme that guarantees a prescribed convergence rate for micromirror systems, independent of precise model knowledge. By integrating a rate factor into the cost function, the proposed approach transforms the optimal tracking problem into one that enforces the tracking error to decay faster than a pre-specified exponential rate. To solve the resulting algebraic Riccati equation efficiently, a modified hybrid iteration learning algorithm is developed. This algorithm overcomes the slow convergence associated with value iteration and eliminates the requirement for an initial stabilizing control policy inherent in policy iteration. A distinguishing feature of this work is a novel data matrix construction method that filters out iteration-irrelevant terms, thereby decoupling the learning process from exosystem states. This innovation not only reduces computational burden but also relaxes the rank conditions required for data collection. Theoretical analysis proves that the learned controller guarantees asymptotic tracking and the desired convergence speed. Extensive simulations validate the effectiveness of the scheme, demonstrating that it achieves highprecision tracking with superior convergence efficiency compared to existing reinforcement learning methods.

KEYWORDS

Adaptive dynamic programming, data-driven control, guaranteed convergence rate, micromirror, optimal output tracking.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Electrostatic torsional micromirrors constitute a cornerstone technology in Micro-Opto-Electro-Mechanical Systems (MOEMS), enabling critical applications ranging from digital communication and high-fidelity imaging to biomedical displays $[1, 2]$ . In these high-precision scenarios, the control objective is to ensure that the micromirror tracks reference trajectories with exceptional accuracy and rapid response speeds. This challenge is formally known as the output tracking problem. While general output regulation theory can solve this problem $[3, 4]$ , it typically focuses on asymptotic stability without explicitly optimizing transient performance.

Furthermore, as noted in $[5]$ , electrostatic micromirrors impose stringent requirements on transient performance. To address this, the tracking problem can be formulated as an optimal control problem by minimizing a specific cost function. This extended formulation is referred to as the optimal output regulation or optimal output tracking problem, and solutions to it have been investigated by several researchers $[6, 7]$ .

However, standard model-based approaches necessitate precise knowledge of the system dynamics $[8, 9, 10]$ . As highlighted in recent studies $[11, 12]$ , Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems (MEMS) devices are inherently susceptible to fabrication inconsistencies and environmental fluctuations. These intrinsic imperfections introduce parametric uncertainties, precluding the efficacy of traditional model-based control strategies.

Various traditional adaptive control techniques are effective in addressing parametric uncertainty $[13, 14, 15]$ . However, they often fall short in guaranteeing system optimality. Recently, Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Adaptive Dynamic Programming (ADP) have emerged as powerful tools for solving optimal control problems $[16, 17, 18, 19]$ . Integrating with classical output regulation theory, RL techniques have been extended to address the optimal output regulation problem for systems with unknown models. For example, Gao and Jiang $[20]$ solved the optimal output regulation problem using a feedforward design method, which necessitates solving both the Algebraic Riccati Equation (ARE) and regulator equations. Subsequently, Liu and Gao $[21]$ employed the Policy Iteration (PI) method combined with the internal model principle to solve the problem, eliminating the requirement to solve regulator equations for unknown models. Among learning strategies aiming to approximate the ARE solution, PI and Value Iteration (VI) are the two primary iterative approaches. While PI typically yields quadratic convergence, it necessitates an initial stabilizing control policy. To relax this strict condition, VI methods have been proposed $[22, 23]$ . However, VI generally requires a large number of iterations to approximate the optimal gain. To overcome these limitations, Gao $[24]$ proposed a Hybrid Iteration (HI) algorithm that removes the need for an initial stabilizing policy while achieving faster convergence than VI.

Despite these advancements, a critical challenge remains: certain applications of MOEMS, such as high-speed optical switching, demand a guaranteed convergence rate for the tracking error. The studies mentioned above generally do not explicitly address this requirement. Recent works $[25, 26]$ have utilized VI and PI methods to solve the optimal output tracking problem with an assured convergence rate but still face the inherent limitations of the PI or VI algorithms. In this paper, a data-driven optimal output tracking scheme is proposed for micromirror systems with a guaranteed convergence rate. By proposing a modified Hybrid Iteration framework, the challenges of model uncertainty, convergence speed, and algorithmic efficiency are simultaneously addressed. The main contributions of this

work are as follows:

1) A data-driven optimal control scheme is formulated for the micromirror system. In contrast to existing schemes $[2, 27]$ , the optimal output tracking problem is solved using only system data, eliminating the need for precise system identification.

2) Unlike the approaches in [24, 28, 29, 30], the convergence rate of the tracking error is explicitly ensured, guaranteeing that it decays faster than a prescribed exponential rate $e^{-\gamma t}$ .

3) A modified Hybrid Iteration algorithm is developed to overcome the deficiencies of VI and PI. Distinct from [24], a novel data matrix construction method is proposed that filters out iteration-irrelevant terms. This modification reduces the computational load, lowers the required rank condition for data collection, and decouples the learning algorithm from the exosystem state v.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the mathematical model of the micromirror system and formulates the optimal output regulation problem with a guaranteed convergence rate. Section 3 details the development of the data-driven control scheme, including the derivation of the modified Hybrid Iteration algorithm and the corresponding theoretical analysis. Section 4 provides simulation results to validate the effectiveness and superiority of the proposed method. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper.

## 2 | PROBLEM STATEMENT AND FORMULATION

First, the system model of the micromirror is introduced, which is given by [1]

$$
(J _ {1} + J _ {2}) \ddot {\alpha} + D _ {1} \dot {\alpha} + M _ {1} \alpha = T _ {\alpha},\tag{1}
$$

$$
J _ {1} \ddot {\beta} + D _ {2} \dot {\beta} + M _ {2} \beta = T _ {\beta},
$$

where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ represent the tilt angles of x-axis and y-axis, $J_{1}$ and $J_{2}$ denote the mass moments of inertia of the mirror plate and gimbal, respectively, $D_{1}$ and $D_{2}$ represent the damping coefficients, $M_{1}$ , $M_{2}$ represent the stiffness coefficients, and $T_{\alpha}$ and $T_{\beta}$ are electrostatic torques. To unify the order of magnitude of the variables, this paper introduces the parameter $\ell = \omega_{0}t$ , $\omega_{0} = \sqrt{M_{2}/J_{1}}$ , and let $x_{1} = d\alpha/d\ell$ , $x_{2} = \alpha$ , $x_{3} = d\beta/d\ell$ , and $x_{4} = \beta$ . For notational brevity, the time variable t is used to denote the dimensionless time $\ell$ in the remainder of this paper. The scanning electron micrograph (SEM) image of micromirror is shown in Fig. 1.

As a result, system (1) can be described as [1]

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \dot {x} _ {1} = - R _ {\alpha} x _ {1} - \lambda_ {\alpha \beta} x _ {2} + G _ {\alpha} T _ {\alpha}, \\ \dot {x} _ {2} = x _ {1}, \\ y _ {\alpha} = x _ {2}, \end{array} \right. \quad \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \dot {x} _ {3} = - R _ {\beta} x _ {3} - x _ {4} + G _ {\beta} T _ {\beta}, \\ \dot {x} _ {4} = x _ {3}, \\ y _ {\beta} = x _ {4}, \end{array} \right..\tag{2}
$$

where the parameters are $R_{\alpha} = D_{1}/((J_{1} + J_{2})\omega_{0}), R_{\beta} = D_{2}/(J_{1}\omega_{0}), \lambda_{\alpha\beta} = \omega_{1}^{2}/\omega_{0}^{2}, \omega_{1} = \sqrt{M_{1}/(J_{1} + J_{2})}, G_{\alpha} = 1/((J_{1} + J_{2})\omega_{0}^{2})$ , and $G_{\beta} = 1/M_{2}$ .

The system is characterized as an uncertain plant where the system dynamic coefficients J, D, and M are all explicitly treated as unknown parameters subject to variations. Consequently, the parameters $R_{\alpha}$ , $R_{\beta}$ , $\lambda_{\alpha\beta}$ , $G_{\alpha}$ , and $G_{\beta}$ are considered to be unknown. Obviously, the $\alpha$ and $\beta$ axes share the same general system structure as follows.

FIGURE 1 The scanning electron micrograph (SEM) image of the micromirror.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \dot {x} = A x + B u \\ y = C x. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

For the convenience of subsequent analysis, only the $\alpha$ system is rewritten in the following compact form:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left[ \begin{array}{c} \dot {x} _ {1} \\ \dot {x} _ {2} \end{array} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} - R _ {\alpha} & - \lambda_ {\alpha \beta} \\ 1 & 0 \end{array} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} x _ {1} \\ x _ {2} \end{array} \right] + \left[ \begin{array}{c} G _ {\alpha} / \kappa_ {\alpha} \\ 0 \end{array} \right] u _ {\alpha} \triangleq A _ {\alpha} x _ {\alpha} + B _ {\alpha} u _ {\alpha} \\ y _ {\alpha} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} 0 & 1 \end{array} \right] x _ {\alpha} \triangleq C _ {\alpha} x _ {\alpha}, \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where $x_{\alpha} = [x_{1}, x_{2}]^{T}$ is the state vector, $A_{\alpha} = \begin{bmatrix} -R_{\alpha} & -\lambda_{\alpha\beta} \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ , $B_{\alpha} = \begin{bmatrix} G_{\alpha}/\kappa_{\alpha} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ , and $C_{\alpha} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ . The relationships between the control inputs and the generated electrostatic torques are approximated as linear functions $u_{\alpha} = \kappa_{\alpha} T_{\alpha}$ , where $\kappa_{\alpha}$ is the corresponding linear coefficient.

By the output regulation theory [4], the reference signal is considered to be generated by the following exosystem:

$$
\dot {v} = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \dot {v} _ {1} \\ \dot {v} _ {2} \end{array} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} 0 & 1 \\ - a & 0 \end{array} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} v _ {1} \\ v _ {2} \end{array} \right] \triangleq S v.\tag{5}
$$

Let the reference signal be $ref(t) = -Fv(t)$ . The output error is defined as:

$$
e (t) = C x (t) + F v (t).\tag{6}
$$

Remark 1: The exosystem (5) provides a unified framework for generating diverse reference trajectories. When the parameter is set to $a = \sigma^{2}$ , the system yields sinusoidal signals $v_{1}(t) = A_{m} \sin(\sigma t + \phi)$ , with the amplitude and phase determined by the initial state $[v_{1}(0), v_{2}(0)]^{T} = [A_{m} \sin \phi, \sigma A_{m} \cos \phi]^{T}$ . In the case $a = 0$ , the output becomes a ramp signal $v_{1}(t) = c_{1}t + c_{2}$ , governed by $[v_{1}(0), v_{2}(0)]^{T} = [c_{2}, c_{1}]^{T}$ . The formulation naturally encompasses constant reference signals (step inputs) when $c_{1} = 0$ . Moreover, it allows for the construction of triangular waves by concatenating ramp signals with alternating slopes. Then the tracking control problem can be stated as follows.

Problem 1: Design a dynamic state feedback controller u of the following form:

$$
u = - K _ {x} x - K _ {z} z\tag{7}
$$

$$
\dot {z} = S z + G _ {2} e.
$$

such that the closed-loop system (3)-(7) is asymptotically tracking and the output error $e(t)$ converges to zero as t approaches infinity for all initial conditions. In practice, the micromirror system usually requires a fast enough convergence rate. Therefore, a rate requirement of the tracking error is set as follows:

$$
\lim _ {t \to \infty} e ^ {\gamma t}   e (t) = 0.\tag{8}
$$

Remark 2: The parameter $\gamma \geq 0$ is a given convergence rate criterion. It means that the tracking error $e(t)$ converges to zero with a rate faster than $e^{-\gamma t}$ .

According to the internal model principle [4], in the controller (7) defined above, the equation

$$
\dot {z} = S z + G _ {2} e\tag{9}
$$

is called an internal model for the system (3) and the exosystem (5). To begin with, a matrix $G_{2} \in R^{2 \times 1}$ is chosen such that the pair $(S, G_{2})$ is controllable.

To solve Problem 1, the following assumptions are needed:

Assumption 1 S has no eigenvalues with negative real parts.

Assumption 2 The pair $(A, B)$ is stabilizable.

Assumption 3 rank $\left[ \begin{array}{cc}A - \lambda I & B\\ C & 0 \end{array} \right] = 3,\forall \lambda \in \sigma (S).$

Define an augmented state vector $\eta(t) = \begin{bmatrix} x(t) \\ z(t) \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{4}$ . Combining (3) and (9), the dynamics of the augmented system can be expressed as:

$$
\dot {\eta} (t) = \bar {A} \eta (t) + \bar {B} u (t) + \bar {D} v (t).\tag{10}
$$

where

$$
\bar {A} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} A & 0 \\ G _ {2} C & S \end{array} \right], \quad \bar {B} = \left[ \begin{array}{c} B \\ 0 \end{array} \right], \quad \bar {D} = \left[ \begin{array}{c} 0 \\ G _ {2} F \end{array} \right].
$$

The tracking error $e(t)$ can now be rewritten in terms of the augmented state as:

$$
e (t) = \bar {C} \eta (t) + F v (t),\tag{11}
$$

where $\bar{C} = \begin{bmatrix} C & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ . Applying the state-feedback control law $u = -\bar{K}\eta$ , where $\bar{K} = \begin{bmatrix} K_x & K_z \end{bmatrix}$ , to the augmented system (10) yields the closed-loop system:

$$
\dot {\eta} (t) = (\bar {A} - \bar {B} \bar {K}) \eta (t) + \bar {D} v (t).\tag{12}
$$

Lemma 1 Under Assumptions 1-3, according to Lemma 1.26 of [4], the pair $(\bar{A},\bar{B})$ is stabilizable with the internal model. By Remark 1.28 of [4], if $\bar{K}$ is chosen such that $\bar{A} -\bar{B}\bar{K}$ is Hurwitz, then the system (3)-(6) with controller (7) solves Problem 1.

Proof According to Lemma 1.27 of [4], the following regulator equations have a unique solution $(X, Z)$ :

$$
\begin{array}{c} X S = A X - B K _ {x} X - B K _ {z} Z \\ Z S = S Z + G _ {2} (C X + F) \\ 0 = C X + F. \end{array}\tag{13}
$$

The error states are defined as: $\bar{x} = x - Xv$ , $\bar{z} = z - Zv$ . Then let $\bar{u} = u - Uv$ with $U = -K_{x}X - K_{z}Z$ . Then, the following error system is obtained:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \dot {\tilde {x}} = A \tilde {x} + B \tilde {u} \\ \dot {\tilde {z}} = S \tilde {z} + G _ {2} C \tilde {x} \\ e = C \tilde {x}. \end{array}\tag{14}
$$

Define $\xi = \text{col}(\tilde{x}, \tilde{z})$ . Then (14) can be transformed as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \dot {\xi} = (\bar {A} - \bar {B} \bar {K}) \xi \\ e = \bar {C} \xi . \end{array}\tag{15}
$$

If $\bar{A} -\bar{B}\bar{K}$ is Hurwitz, then $\lim_{t\to \infty}\xi (t) = 0$ and $\lim_{t\to \infty}e(t) = 0$ . Then the proof is completed.

In order to satisfy the requirement of convergence rate, $\xi$ and e are transformed to new variables, which means $\xi_{\gamma} = e^{\gamma t} \xi$ and $e_{\gamma} = e^{\gamma t} e$ . Then the system (14) can be transformed as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \dot {\xi} _ {\gamma} = \bar {A} _ {\gamma} \xi_ {\gamma} + \bar {B} \tilde {u} _ {\gamma} \\ e _ {\gamma} = \bar {C} \xi_ {\gamma}, \end{array}\tag{16}
$$

where $\bar{A}_{\gamma} = \bar{A} + \gamma I_{4}, \bar{u}_{\gamma} = -\bar{K}\xi_{\gamma}$ . According to Lemma 1, the pair $(\bar{A}, \bar{B})$ is stabilizable with the internal model. Based on the Popov-Belevitch-Hautus (PBH) test, $\text{rank}([\bar{A} - \lambda I_{4}, \bar{B}]) = 4$ . Then, $\text{rank}([\bar{A}_{\gamma} - (\lambda + \gamma) I_{4}, \bar{B}]) = 4$ is obtained. Therefore, the pair $(\bar{A}_{\gamma}, \bar{B})$ is stabilizable. Moreover, to improve the transient control performance, the optimal tracking control problem is further considered as follows.

Problem 2: Design a dynamic state feedback controller u of the form (7) such that the closed-loop system (3)-(7) is asymptotically tracking and the output error $e(t)$ converges to zero as t approaches infinity for all initial conditions, while minimizing the following cost function:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \min _ {\tilde {u}} J (\xi_ {\gamma}, \tilde {u} _ {\gamma}) = \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} (\xi_ {\gamma} ^ {T} Q \xi_ {\gamma} + \tilde {u} _ {\gamma} ^ {T} R \tilde {u} _ {\gamma}) d t \\ \text { subject   to } \dot {\xi} _ {\gamma} = \bar {A} _ {\gamma} \xi_ {\gamma} + \bar {B} \tilde {u} _ {\gamma}, \end{array}\tag{17}
$$

where $Q = Q^T \geq 0, R = R^T > 0$ with $(\sqrt{Q}, \bar{A}_{\gamma})$ observable.

## 3 | MAIN RESULTS

Obviously, the dynamic programming problem in Problem 2 is a standard linear quadratic regulator (LQR) problem. The solution of the problem is an optimal state feedback control law of the form $\tilde{u}_{\gamma} = -\bar{K}^{*}\tilde{\xi}_{\gamma}$ , where $\bar{K}^{*} = R^{-1}\bar{B}^{T}P^{*}$ and $P^{*} = P^{*T} > 0$ is the unique positive definite solution of the following algebraic Riccati equation:

$$
\bar {A} _ {\gamma} ^ {T} P ^ {*} + P ^ {*} \bar {A} _ {\gamma} - P ^ {*} \bar {B} R ^ {- 1} \bar {B} ^ {T} P ^ {*} + Q = 0.\tag{18}
$$

The ARE (18) is inherently nonlinear, solving $P^{*}$ from it directly is difficult. To address this, the Kleinman iteration algorithm [31], a well-established numerical method for approximating $P^{*}$ , is employed. This iterative approach ensures convergence under stabilizability conditions and is computationally efficient for solving the ARE, as presented in Algorithm 1.

However, this method requires the knowledge of the system matrices A and B, which may not be available in practice. Therefore, a method is needed to learn the optimal control gain without explicit system identification, which is derived using system data. Data-driven algorithms to solve the optimal output regulation problem have been investigated in several studies. For example, Liu and Gao [21] proposed a PI algorithm to get the $K^{*}$ with a high convergence rate. However, this algorithm requires a stabilizing initial control gain $\bar{K}_{0}$ , which is difficult to obtain in practice. Bian and Jiang [22] further proposed a VI algorithm to eliminate the requirement of an initial stabilizing control gain. However, the convergence rate of the algorithm is much slower than the PI method. To overcome the limitations of both methods, a modified HI algorithm is proposed to solve this problem.

Different from the method of establishing the data equation in [24], a new way is proposed to gather data in the learning stage. Inspired by [26], it is found that the optimal control gain $\bar{K}^{*}$ only depends on the pair $(\bar{A}_{\gamma}, \bar{B})$ . Therefore,

$$
\dot {z} _ {1} = S z _ {1} + G _ {2} y\tag{21}
$$

is introduced to replace (9) in learning stage, yielding

$$
\dot {\zeta} = \bar {A} \zeta + \bar {B} u _ {1}\tag{22}
$$

$$
y = \bar {C} \zeta ,
$$

where $\zeta = \operatorname{col}(x(t), z_{1}(t))$ , and $u_{1} = -\bar{K}\zeta + \mu$ . $\mu$ represents the bounded exploration noise. Similarly, by introducing the convergence rate transformation, the following is obtained:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \dot {\zeta} _ {\gamma} = \bar {A} _ {\gamma} \zeta_ {\gamma} + \bar {B} u _ {1 \gamma} \\ y _ {\gamma} = \bar {C} \zeta_ {\gamma}, \end{array}\tag{23}
$$

where $\zeta_{\gamma}=e^{yr}t\zeta$ , and $u_{1\gamma}=e^{yr}u_{1}$ . It is evident that (23) shares the same matrix pair $(\bar{A}_{\gamma},\bar{B})$ as (16). Therefore, the data of system (10) can be used to learn the optimal control gain $\bar{K}^{*}$ for system (16).

To implement the data-driven algorithm, the data of the system (23) is used to construct the data equation. For any integer $s \in N_{+}$ , let

$$
\begin{array}{c} \delta_ {\zeta_ {\gamma}} = [ \operatorname{vecv} (\zeta_ {\gamma} (t _ {1})) - \operatorname{vecv} (\zeta_ {\gamma} (t _ {0})), \\ \operatorname{vecv} (\zeta_ {\gamma} (t _ {2})) - \operatorname{vecv} (\zeta_ {\gamma} (t _ {1})), \ldots , \\ \operatorname{vecv} (\zeta_ {\gamma} (t _ {s})) - \operatorname{vecv} (\zeta_ {\gamma} (t _ {s - 1})) ] ^ {T} \\ \Gamma_ {\zeta_ {\gamma} \zeta_ {\gamma}} = [ \int_ {t _ {0}} ^ {t _ {1}} \operatorname{vecv} (\zeta_ {\gamma} (\tau)) d \tau , \ldots , \int_ {t _ {s - 1}} ^ {t _ {s}} \operatorname{vecv} (\zeta_ {\gamma} (\tau)) d \tau ] ^ {T} \\ \Gamma_ {\zeta_ {\gamma} \otimes \zeta_ {\gamma}} = [ \int_ {t _ {0}} ^ {t _ {1}} \zeta_ {\gamma} (\tau) \otimes \zeta_ {\gamma} (\tau) d \tau , \ldots , \int_ {t _ {s - 1}} ^ {t _ {s}} \zeta_ {\gamma} (\tau) \otimes \zeta_ {\gamma} (\tau) d \tau ] ^ {T} \\ \Gamma_ {\zeta_ {\gamma} \otimes u _ {1 \gamma}} = [ \int_ {t _ {0}} ^ {t _ {1}} \zeta_ {\gamma} (\tau) \otimes (R u _ {1 \gamma} (\tau)) d \tau , \int_ {t _ {1}} ^ {t _ {2}} \zeta_ {\gamma} (\tau) \otimes (R u _ {1 \gamma} (\tau)) d \tau , \ldots , \\ \int_ {t _ {s - 1}} ^ {t _ {s}} \zeta_ {\gamma} (\tau) \otimes (R u _ {1 \gamma} (\tau)) d \tau ] ^ {T}. \end{array}\tag{24}
$$

According to the VI algorithm [22], the value matrix and control gain are updated by

$$
P _ {i + 1} = \epsilon (\bar {A} _ {\gamma} ^ {T} P _ {i} + P _ {i} \bar {A} _ {\gamma} - P _ {i} \bar {B} R ^ {- 1} \bar {B} ^ {T} P _ {i} + Q) + P _ {i}
$$

$$
\bar {K} _ {i} = R ^ {- 1} \bar {B} ^ {T} P _ {i},\tag{25}
$$

where $\epsilon > 0$ is the step size specified later. Define $V(\zeta_{\gamma}) = \zeta_{\gamma}^{T} P_{i} \zeta_{\gamma}$ , yielding

$$
\begin{array}{l} \zeta_ {\gamma} (t _ {1}) ^ {T} P _ {i} \zeta_ {\gamma} (t _ {1}) - \zeta_ {\gamma} (t _ {0}) ^ {T} P _ {i} \zeta_ {\gamma} (t _ {0}) \\ = \int_ {t _ {0}} ^ {t _ {1}} \zeta_ {\gamma} ^ {T} (\tau) \Xi_ {i} \zeta_ {\gamma} (\tau) + 2 u _ {1 \gamma} ^ {T} R \bar {K} _ {i} \zeta_ {\gamma} (\tau) d \tau , \end{array}\tag{26}
$$

where $\Xi_{i} = \bar{A}_{\gamma}^{T} P_{i} + P_{i} \bar{A}_{\gamma}$ . Then equation (26) with (24) implies the following matrix equation

$$
\Psi_ {V I} ^ {i} \left[ \begin{array}{c} \mathsf {v e c s} (\Xi_ {i}) \\ \mathsf {v e c} (\bar {K} _ {i}) \end{array} \right] = \Phi_ {V I} ^ {i},\tag{27}
$$

where $\Psi_{VI}^{i} = [\Gamma_{\zeta_{Y}\zeta_{Y}}, 2\Gamma_{\zeta_{Y}\otimes u_{1Y}}], \Phi_{VI}^{i} = \delta_{\zeta_{Y}} \operatorname{vecs}(P_i)$ .

To construct the data equation for PI algorithm, this paper rewrites the system (23) as follows:

$$
\dot {\zeta} _ {\gamma} = \bar {A} _ {\gamma} ^ {i} \zeta_ {\gamma} + \bar {B} (\bar {K} _ {i} \zeta_ {\gamma} + u _ {1 \gamma}),\tag{28}
$$

where $\bar{A}_{\gamma}^{i} = \bar{A}_{\gamma} - \bar{B}\bar{K}_{i}$ . By (19), (20) and function V defined above, the following is obtained:

$$

Lemma 3 As proved in Theorem 3 of [24], the sequence $\{P_k\}$ and $\{\bar{K}_k\}$ generated by Algorithm 2 converge to the optimal solutions $P^{*}$ and $\bar{K}^{*}$ , quadratically.

Lemma 3 establishes the convergence of the proposed HI algorithm, demonstrating that it effectively utilizes online data to approximate the optimal control gain $\bar{K}^{*}$ . With the converged optimal gain, the optimal controller can be synthesized to address the optimal tracking control problem defined in Problem 2. The main result is formally stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1 Consider the Micromirror System (3) with the exosystem (5), under Assumptions 1-3. Let $\vec{K}^{*} = [K_{x}^{*}, K_{z}^{*}]$ be the optimal gain obtained from Algorithm 2. The dynamic feedback controller $u = -K_{x}^{*}x - K_{z}^{*}z$ solves Problem 2. Specifically, the tracking error $e(t)$ converges to zero with a rate faster than $\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t}$ while minimizing the cost function (17).

Proof First, according to Lemma 3, the sequence $\{\bar{K}_i\}$ generated by Algorithm 2 converges to the optimal control gain $\bar{K}^*$ . The closed-loop matrix of the $\gamma$ -shifted error system (16) under the optimal control $\tilde{u}_{\gamma} = -\bar{K}^{*}\xi_{\gamma}$ is given by $\bar{A}_{\gamma} - \bar{B}\bar{K}^{*}$ . Since the pair $(\bar{A}_{\gamma},\bar{B})$ is stabilizable and the weighting matrices $Q$ and $R$ satisfy the standard LQR conditions, the optimal gain $\bar{K}^*$ ensures that $\bar{A}_{\gamma} - \bar{B}\bar{K}^*$ is Hurwitz.

Consequently, the state $\xi_{\gamma}(t)$ of the system (16) converges to zero asymptotically, i.e., $\lim_{t\to\infty}\xi_{\gamma}(t)=0$ . Recall the definition $\xi(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t}\xi_{\gamma}(t)$ , which implies $\|\xi(t)\|=\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t}\|\xi_{\gamma}(t)\|$ . Since $\|\xi_{\gamma}(t)\|$ is bounded and decays to zero, it follows that $\xi(t)$ converges to zero with a rate faster than $e^{-\gamma t}$ . Given that the tracking error is $e(t)=\bar{C}\xi(t)$ , we have $\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\gamma t}e(t)=0$ , which satisfies the prescribed convergence rate requirement.

Next, it is shown that the controller $u = -K_x^* x - K_z^* z$ solves the output regulation problem. Observe that the matrix relation holds:

$$
\bar {A} _ {\gamma} - \bar {B} \bar {K} ^ {*} = (\bar {A} + \gamma I) - \bar {B} \bar {K} ^ {*} = (\bar {A} - \bar {B} \bar {K} ^ {*}) + \gamma I.\tag{33}
$$

Let $\lambda_{i}(\cdot)$ denote the eigenvalues of a matrix. The stability of $\bar{A}_{\gamma} - \bar{B}\bar{K}^{*}$ implies that $\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_i(\bar{A} -\bar{B}\bar{K}^*) + \gamma) < 0$ for all $i$ . Therefore, $\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_i(\bar{A} -\bar{B}\bar{K}^*)) < -\gamma$ . Since $\gamma >0$ , the matrix $\bar{A} -\bar{B}\bar{K}^{*}$ is Hurwitz.

Finally, according to Lemma 1, since the controller incorporates an internal model and the resulting closed-loop system matrix $\bar{A} -\bar{B}\bar{K}^{*}$ is Hurwitz, the controller $u = -\bar{K}^{*}\eta$ automatically achieves asymptotic output tracking. The proof is thus completed.

## 4 | SIMULATION

In this section, numerical simulations are conducted on a MEMS micromirror system to verify the effectiveness and superiority of the proposed data-driven optimal output regulation scheme. The physical parameters of the micromirror are selected as follows: $R_{\alpha} = 0.16$ , $R_{\beta} = 0.16$ , $\lambda_{\alpha\beta} = 0.2251$ , $G_{\alpha} = 3.0827 \times 10^{6}$ , and $G_{\beta} = 1.7894 \times 10^{7}$ . To ensure numerical stability during the learning process, scaling factors $\kappa_{\alpha} = 10^{6}$ and $\kappa_{\beta} = 10^{7}$ are introduced. In the simulation setup, the weighting matrices are selected as Q = I₄ and R = 1. The exosystem matrix is set to S = [0,1; -1,0], generating a sinusoidal reference signal with unit frequency. The initial states are set as $x(0) = [0,0]^{T}$ , $z(0) = [0,0]^{T}$ , and $v(0) = [1,0]^{T}$ . To satisfy the rank condition for the learning algorithm, an exploration noise composed of sinusoidal signals with distinct frequencies, given by $u_{noise} = 0.01 \sum_{k=1}^{100} \sin(kt)$ , is added to the control input during the data collection phase. For convenience, only the x-axis system is considered in the following simulations, as the y-axis system can be treated similarly.

To evaluate the capability of the proposed method in guaranteeing the convergence rate, a comparative study is conducted under two different performance criteria: a prescribed convergence rate of $\gamma = 0.4$ and a standard regulation case with $\gamma = 0$ .

The tracking performance results of the x-axis system are presented in Figs. 2 to 5. Figs. 2 and 4 illustrate the trajectories of the system output $y(t)$ and the reference signal $ref(t)$ . It can be intuitively observed from Fig. 2 that under the proposed scheme with $\gamma = 0.4$ , the system output tracks the reference signal rapidly and smoothly. In contrast, as shown in Fig. 4, the standard control ( $\gamma = 0$ ) exhibits a slower response.

To provide a more precise analysis of the transient performance, the tracking error responses are plotted in Fig. 3 and Fig. 5. Fig. 3 demonstrates that with $\gamma = 0.4$ , the tracking error converges to zero faster than the case of $\gamma = 0$ shown in Fig. 5. This comparison strongly validates that the introduction of the rate factor $\gamma$ into the optimization objective effectively accelerates the error convergence, ensuring the high-speed scanning requirements of micromirror systems are met.

## 5 | CONCLUSION

This paper proposes a data-driven optimal output regulation scheme for uncertain micromirror systems, ensuring a prescribed convergence rate via a rate-weighted performance index. A modified Hybrid Iteration (HI) algorithm is developed, featuring a novel data matrix construction that decouples the learning process from exosystem states, thereby reducing computational complexity. Theoretical analysis and simulations confirm that the proposed method achieves high-precision tracking with the desired convergence speed, offering a superior balance of efficiency and feasibility compared to traditional Value Iteration and Policy Iteration methods.
